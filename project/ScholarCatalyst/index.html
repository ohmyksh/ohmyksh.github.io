Abstract

What makes great scientists great? Even as AI systems start to make progress on open problems, scientists remain far ahead of them at sensing which prior idea, buried in an ever-growing archive of research, a new problem needs. To study this skill, we draw on researchers who know firsthand which earlier work advanced their completed projects, with papers serving as pointers to the ideas within. Using our automated pipeline that makes author annotation scalable, we build ScholarCatalyst by having 184 lead authors of 207 recent computer science papers label which candidates did or could have advanced their project, each with a detailed rationale. We introduce a retrieval task with author-provided judgments: given an initial research question, retrieve these papers from only the literature available when the project began. Agentic search does no better than embedding retrieval (0.42 vs. 0.48 Recall@20) despite calling that same retriever as a tool. Even an agent built on Claude Fable 5.1, which may have seen the completed papers during training, reaches only 0.51 R@20. These results highlight the need for new training recipes that equip models with expert intuition for searching broad corpora. We envision ScholarCatalyst as a step toward scientific agents that can take a half-formed idea and point to the prior research it needs.

Catalyst Paper Retrieval for Early-Stage Research Questions

Scientific research is inherently cumulative, with each project building on previous results and ideas. Current AI systems for research mainly accelerate progress on an already well-defined subproblem, where the remaining work is closer to engineering. Deciding which existing idea a new problem should build on is still mostly left to human researchers. An insightful prior paper can turn a vague question into a well-defined problem, for instance by revealing a limitation that motivates a new direction or suggesting an approach worth adapting, thereby shaping and accelerating (catalyzing) subsequent research. Making this choice well is a defining ability of great scientists, and a critical part of what researchers call research taste.

We ask whether models can make that decision through a concrete task: given an early stage research question, find its catalyst papers, by which we mean prior papers whose ideas did or could have meaningfully advanced the project. A system searches a temporally filtered corpus and is evaluated against the catalyst papers identified by the source paper authors.

Temporal cutoff Systems search only papers published before the source paper. The source paper is withheld and web search is disabled, so the task reflects the literature available while the research was still in progress.

Which is a catalyst paper?

Main Results

Current retrievers miss most of the papers that inspired a project. General purpose dense retrievers perform best, yet place 39% of author identified inspiration papers in the top 20 for Core Research Queries and 51% for Subfield specific Queries. Agentic search does not improve over embedding retrieval.

Our Data

Each completed source paper contributes one Core Research Query and one or more Subfield specific Queries. For every query, authors identify catalyst papers that offered useful ideas, distinguish topically related hard negatives, and provide a rationale for each judgment. Authors review and revise all of these materials.

Data Collection Pipeline

  • Automated construction Starting from one source paper arXiv ID, the pipeline parses the paper, resolves its references, proposes a Core Research Query and Subfield specific Queries, then retrieves candidate papers from the temporally appropriate corpus.
  • Author validation and revision Authors review every proposed query and candidate paper. They validate, revise, or rewrite the materials, mark catalyst papers and hard negatives, and explain each relevance judgment.

Analysis

Inspiration is diverse and not captured by similarity

An LLM judge classifies 663 key inspiration rationales under a nine type taxonomy. Authors most often credit a technique they adapted, empirical findings that supported a direction, an idea extended to a new setting, or a limitation that motivated a new approach. More than one type applies to 55.8% of rationales. Hard negatives are at least as similar to the query as catalyst papers under both lexical and embedding similarity.

Citation status does not identify inspiration

Source paper citations provide an incomplete signal of inspiration. 43.6% of Subfield specific catalyst papers are not cited by the source paper, and 67.8% of those uncited papers share no references with it. Among cited catalyst papers, only 46.2% for Core Research Queries and 34.0% for Subfield specific Queries carry a uses or extends citation intent label.

Recognition depends on candidate coverage

To separate retrieval coverage from recognition performance, we apply LLM based reranking to the top 20 candidates from Gemini Embedding 2 for 50 Core Research Queries and 50 Subfield specific Queries. We compare reranking with and without gold injection, which replaces negative candidates with missing catalyst papers while keeping the pool size fixed. Stronger rerankers gain more in Recall at 5 from gold injection.

Frontier model comparison

Claude Fable 5.1 is reported separately as a comparison model. In the full benchmark, its agents reach 0.51 to 0.53 Recall at 20 on Core Research Queries. On Subfield specific Queries, the best Fable agent reaches 0.50 compared with 0.51 for the strongest embedding retriever.

Outro

Recent work on reasoning-intensive retrieval evaluates relevance relations that require reasoning to recognize. ScholarCatalyst pushes further, requiring judgments of how prior ideas could advance a research project.

We see two milestones ahead. The first is expert-level retrieval, matching a domain expert's judgment within their own field. The second is retrieval beyond any individual expert: being able to exercise that level of intuition across a breadth of fields no single researcher can follow.

This breadth may be where such systems help most, since nearly half of the papers our authors credit (49.5%) come from outside their project's subject area, and 43.6% of subfield-specific positives were never cited in the source paper. Researchers may therefore be limited more by what they have seen than by how well they judge it. The full literature makes this gap far larger than our benchmark shows: Semantic Scholar alone indexes over 225M papers, compared with 190K in our corpus.

What stands between current systems and both milestones is an expert-level sense of which ideas matter, and we hope ScholarCatalyst offers a starting point for developing it. If undiscovered public knowledge (1986) is as common as our results imply, models capable of expert judgment across fields could unlock a large backlog of progress hidden in plain sight.
